DA-004 — Scene 01 — Arrival — Approved Draft v1.1

By the time Eli turned the camera on, rain had sheeted across the Kestrel’s front drive hard enough to turn the headlights of arriving cars into white smears on the pavement. He stood under the porte cochere with his jacket dark at the shoulders and framed the hotel beyond a baggage cart stacked with hard-shell suitcases. Warm windows rose four floors above him. A shuttle idled at the curb while two children in matching raincoats chased each other around their mother’s legs and a bellman tried to keep a garment bag from dragging through a puddle.

Eli checked the red recording icon, lifted the camera a little, and put on the voice that came easiest once a lens was pointed at him.

“Made it to the Kestrel. We’ve got the room for the night, ghost tour in about forty minutes, and after that we’re going to see how long my dad lasts before he decides this was a terrible idea.”

Martin’s voice came from behind him. “I decided that in the car.”

Eli swung the camera around.

His father stood beside the open trunk with a tripod case in one hand and Eli’s equipment bag in the other. Rain blew under the roof and dotted his glasses. He looked into the lens with the patient expression of someone being made to pose for a photograph he had not requested.

“You drove six hours,” Eli said.

“I was promised dinner.”

“That’s your statement?”

“That’s my statement until dinner.”

Eli laughed and lowered the camera. Martin handed him the equipment bag but kept the tripod, then nodded toward the lobby doors.

“Come on. You can make it ominous after we’re dry.”

Inside, the Kestrel was busy enough that Eli had to step aside almost immediately for a family rolling three suitcases toward the elevators. Wet coats gave the lobby a faint wool smell. A conference group occupied the couches beneath a row of old landscape photographs, their plastic name badges turned backward or tucked into shirt pockets now that the day’s sessions were over. Somewhere past the front desk, dishes knocked together in a dining room. The elevator chimed, opened, filled, and shut again. A ceiling vent moved a steady ribbon of warm air across the registration counter.

Eli filmed some of it anyway: the brass mail slots that had survived whatever renovations had put key-card readers on the doors, the worn stair rail, the rain tracking down the tall windows. Nothing about the lobby suggested an investigation site. People were asking for extra towels and directions to the bar.

At check-in, the clerk gave them two key cards and pointed out the elevators, breakfast hours, and the meeting place for the evening tour. Martin listened to all of it. Eli watched the clock over the desk.

“Forty minutes,” he said when they had their bags again.

“Thirty-seven now.”

“You’re getting into it.”

“I’m getting hungry.”

The elevator carried them up with an older couple and a woman holding a sleeping toddler against her shoulder. Eli kept the camera down. The little boy’s sneaker bumped softly against the woman’s coat each time the car slowed. On the upper floor, the doors opened onto a carpeted corridor where a housekeeping cart had been parked in an alcove. A vacuum cleaner sat unplugged beside it. Farther down, somebody laughed behind a closed door.

Martin glanced along the hall. “Terrifying.”

“Save some material for the interview.”

“I have notes.”

Their room was near the end of the corridor. Eli unlocked it and entered first with the camera running again.

The room had been updated without losing all of its older proportions. Two chairs sat near the window with a small round table between them. A dark wooden dresser faced the bed. The bathroom occupied a narrow recess near the entrance, its door opening inward toward a tiled floor. Warm air came through a vent below the window with a low mechanical hiss. Rain tapped against the glass, quieter here than it had been downstairs.

Eli panned slowly across the room, then caught Martin dropping the tripod case beside the dresser.

“Well?” Eli asked.

Martin looked around. “It’s a hotel room.”

“That’s going in the trailer.”

Martin took off his wet jacket and hung it over the back of one of the chairs. “You asked.”

Eli set the camera on the dresser and adjusted it until both of them were in frame. The bathroom door stood open behind Martin. Eli switched on the bathroom light because without it Martin’s face fell into shadow whenever he moved away from the lamp.

“Okay,” Eli said. “Before the tour.”

Martin was unzipping his overnight bag. “Before the tour what?”

“I want one thing tonight.”

“You brought three bags.”

“Not equipment.” Eli rested one hand on the dresser beside the camera. “One thing that happens while we’re both there. Same time, same place. Something you can’t explain away tomorrow.”

Martin stopped with one hand inside the bag. For a moment he looked at Eli rather than the camera.

“If I hear something, I’ll tell you I heard it.”

“I know.”

“And if I can see what caused it, I’m going to tell you that too.”

Eli felt his jaw tighten and made himself unclench it. “I’m not asking you to pretend.”

“Good.” Martin pulled out a folded shirt and set it on the bed. “Then let me see what happens.”

Eli reached over and checked the camera’s battery level instead of answering.

“Just pay attention,” he said.

Martin gave him a brief look. “I came, didn’t I?”

The bathroom door moved behind him.

At first Eli saw it only on the camera screen: the bright vertical strip of bathroom light narrowing by a few inches. He looked past the camera. The door continued inward, slow and steady, until the latch met the strike plate with a small wooden click.

Neither of them spoke.

Martin turned around.

Eli picked up the camera. “You saw that?”

“I saw it close.”

“You didn’t touch it?”

Martin looked back at him. “No.”

Eli crossed the room and opened the door to the same wide angle. He took his hand away.

They waited.

The door stayed open.

He moved it halfway closed and released it. Nothing. He tried again from farther out. The door shifted perhaps half an inch and stopped.

Martin crouched beside the hinge. He pressed a fingertip against the edge of the door, opened it, and let it go. Then he stood and held his palm near the bathroom vent before checking the larger vent under the window.

“Air’s running,” he said.

“Enough to close that?”

“I don’t know.”

Eli stepped into the corridor and pulled the room’s entrance door nearly shut, then opened it again while Martin watched the bathroom door. The interior door did not move. They reversed positions. Martin opened the entrance door. Eli watched the narrow strip of light at the bathroom threshold.

Nothing.

Martin returned to the hinge and ran his thumb along one screw head. “Could be the floor. Could be the hinge. Pressure changed when we came in.”

“But it won’t do it now.”

“No.”

Eli waited for the rest. Martin ran his thumb over the hinge screw again.

Eli aimed the camera at the bathroom door, then at his father. “So?”

“So it closed.”

“And?”

Martin picked up his jacket from the chair. “And we have a ghost tour in thirty minutes.”

Eli almost argued. Instead he looked down at the camera and backed the clip up far enough to watch the door move again. On the small screen, Martin was talking, the bathroom behind him bright and ordinary, and then the line of light narrowed until it disappeared. No hand entered frame. No one crossed the doorway. The microphone had caught the latch clearly.

He watched it once more, then stopped playback.

In his field notebook he wrote the time and, beneath it: BATH DOOR MOVED SHUT WHILE BOTH IN ROOM. Could not reproduce.

He read the line once, then drew a small question mark in the margin.

Martin was already checking the restaurant hours on the card sleeve from the front desk.

At the threshold, Eli looked back. The bathroom door was still open exactly where Martin had left it.

He smiled anyway.

DA-004 — Scene 02 — Public Ghosts — Approved Draft v1.1

By the time the tour group crossed the covered walk to the Kestrel’s old event building, the rain had eased from a downpour to a steady hiss against the roof. Eli kept the camera at chest height while the guide led twelve of them through a side door and waited for the last couple to shake out an umbrella before beginning.

The room beyond had a low stage at one end, stacked chairs against one wall, and a piano under a fitted cover. Exit signs and wall sconces gave the place more light than Eli expected. A custodian’s yellow floor sign stood folded beside a utility closet. Somewhere behind the stage, a fan or blower ran with a soft continuous vibration.

The guide let the group look around before pointing toward a stairwell near the back.

“Most people who come through here have heard about the woman in the concert hall,” he said. “The version I learned says staff found a young woman living downstairs sometime in the seventies. She left, and later people said she died outside during the winter.”

He paused, then added, “You’ll hear it differently depending on who tells it.”

Martin, beside Eli, looked toward the stairwell. “Differently how?”

“Age, mostly. Sometimes the year. Some people give her a name. Some say she was a runaway. I’ve heard enough versions that I just call her the woman from the hall.”

A woman near the front asked whether the hotel had records of her.

The guide shook his head. “Not that I’ve seen.”

Eli kept filming. Martin said nothing else, but when the group moved toward the stairwell he glanced once at Eli’s camera screen, where the guide’s last answer had been captured along with the running time code.

They went downstairs into a basement corridor with painted concrete walls and exposed pipe. The guide pointed out the rooms associated with most of the concert-hall stories. He described footsteps when the building was empty, doors found in different positions, and a woman’s voice reported by guests and employees. At the word doors, Eli glanced at Martin. Martin was already looking at him. Neither said anything, and Eli kept filming. Someone asked where the woman had slept. “Somewhere downstairs,” the guide said. Someone else asked when. “Late seventies, maybe early eighties.”

Back upstairs, the guide turned to Eli. “You’re in the Rider Room tonight, right?”

Eli brought the camera up. “That’s us.”

A few people in the group looked at him.

“The fourth-floor room?” one man asked.

“The one people call the Rider Room,” the guide said. “Guests have reported seeing a man near the bed or hearing heavy steps. Cowboy hat in some versions. Rider in others.”

“Who was he?” Eli asked.

The guide gave a small, apologetic smile. “That depends on which story you bought with your room.”

A few people laughed.

“No, really,” Eli said.

“Really, I don’t know. Some people say cowboy. Some just say rider.”

Martin looked toward Eli. “So we have a room with a nickname.”

“We have a room with reports,” Eli said.

“We have both.”

The guide had already moved on.

In the main hotel again, he gathered them near the elevators and talked about the upper corridors. Children, he said, were one of the most common things guests thought they heard late at night: running, whispering, crying, the bounce of a ball where no child was visible. He said some guests left candy outside their rooms or held it out while recording because the children were supposed to like sweets.

From his jacket pocket he produced a wrapped lollipop and held it toward Eli.

“You wanted to investigate later.”

Eli took it. “This is official equipment?”

“It comes with no warranty.”

Martin looked at the candy in Eli’s hand. “Good. I was worried about calibration.”

Eli laughed. He held the wrapper close to the camera for a second, then slipped the lollipop into the front pocket of his equipment bag.

“People actually get movement with these?” he asked.

“People try them,” the guide said. “Some swear by them.”

Martin gave the guide a small nod.

The tour continued through another public room and back toward the event building. Eli was filming an old framed photograph on the wall when the guide stopped speaking mid-sentence.

He was looking through an open doorway into a narrow side room.

“What?” someone asked.

The guide walked two steps closer but did not enter. A bathroom door stood partly open inside the room. Yellow light showed across the tile beyond it.

“I shut this down before the tour,” he said.

Nobody answered at first.

“The bathroom light?” Eli asked.

“Yeah.” The guide kept looking at it. “I turn the lights off when I close these rooms. I did this one before you all came over.”

Eli raised the camera and moved toward the doorway.

The guide looked at him. “You’re the one who wanted to investigate.”

“You want us to check it?”

“I’m not going in first.”

He said it lightly, but he stayed where he was.

Eli looked at Martin.

Martin had already stepped beside him.

They entered together. The guide remained at the threshold while the rest of the group waited farther back in the hall.

The side room smelled faintly of dust and floor cleaner. The bathroom light was plainly on. Eli framed the open bathroom door, the glowing fixture, and the wall switch in one shot.

“Film the switch before you touch anything,” Martin said.

Eli held the camera steady.

The switch was in the up position.

“You’re sure you turned this one off?” Eli called back.

“I’m sure I shut the room down.”

Eli kept the camera on the switch for another few seconds before looking back toward the guide.

“If somebody’s in here,” he said, “can you turn that light off for us?”

The light stayed on.

They waited through the blower’s vibration and the faint noise of the tour group in the hall.

“Can you close the bathroom door?” Eli asked.

Nothing moved.

Martin stood near the entrance with his hands at his sides. He did not look impatient. He looked at the switch, then the fixture, then the ceiling vent.

Eli tried once more. “Anything you can do so we know you’re here.”

The mechanical hum continued unchanged.

After half a minute Martin reached toward the switch, stopped, and looked at Eli.

“Got it?”

“Got it.”

Martin turned the light off.

The guide exhaled from the doorway. “Thank you.”

Eli swung the camera toward him. “You really didn’t want to come back in here.”

“I’ve had enough weird nights in this building.”

Martin glanced at Eli, but he did not comment on the answer.

As they started out, something moved near the bathroom doorway.

Eli stopped.

Three narrow fabric pulls hung from the edge of a decorative shade beside the window. One was swaying. The other two barely moved.

“Hold on.”

He brought the camera close. The moving strip traveled perhaps an inch in either direction, slowed, then moved again.

Martin came back beside him.

“You touching that?” Eli asked.

“No.”

Eli held his free hand several inches from the fabric. He could not feel a distinct draft there. Martin checked the vent above the bathroom, then the gap beneath the side-room door.

“There’s air moving in the room,” Martin said.

“Right there?”

“I can’t tell.”

Eli watched the strip for another ten seconds. Its motion diminished until it matched the others.

He did not touch it.

On the way back toward the main hall, the guide waited until the rest of the tour group had gone through the connecting doorway before speaking to Eli and Martin again.

“There is one place I can let you look that isn’t on the regular part of the tour.”

He took a key ring from his pocket and led them to a plain door marked EMPLOYEES ONLY.

“Service passage,” he said. “Runs behind part of this building. Pipes, ducts, storage access. Nothing glamorous.”

Eli raised the camera.

The guide put the key in the lock, then stopped with his hand on it.

“I was back here once with another employee,” he said. “We both stopped because I heard somebody say hello behind us. Sounded like a woman. I thought someone had followed us in.”

“Was there anybody there?” Eli asked.

“Not that we found.”

“The woman from downstairs?”

The guide shrugged. “That’s what people like to call it. I heard a voice.”

Martin spoke before Eli could ask anything else.

“Where were you standing?”

The guide turned the key. The lock released with a heavy click.

He opened the door just far enough to point into a narrow passage crossed overhead by pipes and ductwork.

“About halfway down.”

Martin stepped closer to the threshold.

“Show us.”

DA-004 — Scene 03 — Employee Passage — Approved Draft v1.0

The guide opened the staff door wide enough for Martin to pass, then held it for Eli and the camera.

The passage was narrower than it had looked from the threshold. Painted concrete ran underfoot. Pipes crossed the ceiling at different heights, some wrapped in dull insulation, others bare. A rectangular duct occupied most of the space above Eli’s right shoulder. Utility bulbs in wire cages continued down the corridor until a bend cut off the view. Along one wall, shallow recesses held stacked folding tables, cleaning supplies, and two gray bins with lids.

Nothing in the space was quiet.

Air moved steadily through the ductwork. A pipe gave a faint metallic tick somewhere ahead of them. Through the closed staff door behind Eli came the softened movement of the tour group: shoes on the public floor, a cough, someone laughing once before the sound dropped away.

The guide walked ahead and stopped near the bend.

“About here,” he said.

Martin came up beside him. “You were facing which way?”

The guide pointed toward the far end of the passage. “That way. Other employee was next to me. We both turned because I thought somebody had come in behind us.”

Martin looked back toward the door they had entered through, then along the walls.

“And you heard a word?”

“I heard what sounded like hello.”

“What did the other guy hear?”

The guide hesitated. “I couldn’t tell you what he heard.”

Eli kept the camera on him.

Martin nodded once. “Okay.”

Martin gave no sign that the answer had settled anything.

The guide glanced toward the hall behind them. “I can give you a couple minutes. I need to keep the group moving.”

“You’re leaving us in the haunted tunnel?” Eli asked.

“Service passage,” the guide said. “And the door stays open.”

He stepped back toward the entrance. Before he left, Martin asked, “People use this during the day?”

“All the time. Staff, maintenance, storage. Depends what’s going on.”

“Tonight?”

“Hotel’s open.”

Martin looked at Eli.

Eli turned the camera toward himself for a moment. “We’re in the service passage behind the event space. Guide’s at the entrance. Martin and I are about halfway in. There are active hotel spaces around us, pipes and ductwork overhead, and we do not know what connects to what behind the walls.”

“Good,” Martin said.

Eli swung the camera back toward the passage.

The guide remained visible at the far end near the open staff door. The public corridor beyond him appeared as a brighter rectangle. Eli moved a few steps past Martin, stopped before the bend, and held on the empty section ahead.

He held the camera still and recorded ten seconds without speaking.

Air through the duct.

A distant door closing somewhere outside the passage.

A low vibration that Eli could feel more through the soles of his shoes than hear.

Then the ordinary sounds thinned enough that he tried a question.

“If somebody’s back here with us, can you make a sound?”

A short scrape came from somewhere beyond him.

Eli stopped breathing for a second.

Martin’s head came up.

The sound had been brief—something rough shifting across a hard surface, then stopping. Eli had the impression it came from around the bend, but the duct above him carried a faint after-vibration that made the direction hard to hold.

He looked at Martin.

“You heard that?”

Martin raised one hand without looking at him.

They listened.

The guide called quietly from the entrance. “What?”

Neither answered.

Five seconds passed. Maybe eight.

A similar scrape sounded again.

This time Eli turned toward the wall on his left.

Martin turned back toward the entrance.

They looked at each other.

“Where?” Eli whispered.

Martin pointed behind Eli, then stopped halfway through the gesture. “I don’t know.”

Eli moved toward the bend.

Martin passed him before he reached it.

The corridor continued only a short distance before narrowing around more storage recesses and utility lines. Martin checked each visible opening without touching anything. He leaned close to a closed service door, listened, then looked down at the gap beneath it. No light moved there. He pressed his palm against the broad duct along the wall and kept it there for several seconds.

Eli followed with the camera.

“Anything?”

“Air.”

Martin moved to the opposite wall and listened again.

The guide had come partway back into the passage. “There are other staff areas on the other side of some of this. I couldn’t tell you exactly what lines up where without a plan.”

“Anyone working over there?” Martin asked.

“Probably. Not constantly.”

Martin looked toward the ceiling pipes. “Any carts?”

“Could be.”

Martin looked from the service door to the duct, then back toward the entrance. He checked each possible route the sound might have taken before moving on to the next.

A faint thump came through the wall.

All three of them heard that one. The guide looked immediately toward the public side of the building.

“That could be anything,” he said.

Martin nodded. “That one, yes.”

Eli lowered the camera a few inches. “And the scrape?”

Martin looked down the passage again.

“Could be anything too.”

“But you heard it.”

“Yes.”

“Twice.”

“Yes.”

Eli waited.

Martin did not add anything.

The guide returned to the spot where he had described hearing the voice. “It was right around here.”

Eli raised the camera again. “Same place we just heard that?”

“Close.”

“Could that have been what you heard before?”

The guide gave a small shrug. “Maybe. Mine sounded like a person to me.”

Eli turned toward Martin. “What did yours sound like?”

“A scrape.”

“Not somebody moving?”

Martin looked at him. “Something moved, or a sound traveled from something that moved. I don’t know which.”

Eli glanced toward the guide. “Not the woman saying hello.”

“I didn’t hear a woman,” Martin said. “I heard the sound.”

Eli lowered the camera a fraction. Martin held his gaze and did not qualify the answer. For once there was no joke waiting behind it.

Eli checked the recording timer.

“Okay,” he said. “Then when the tour’s done, we go back upstairs. Camera stays fixed. Recorder goes down. We sit still and see what we hear when nobody’s walking around.”

Martin looked at him for a moment.

“No names,” he said.

“What?”

“No cowboy. No children. No woman from the hall. Ask for a sound if you want. Don’t supply the answer in the question.”

Eli looked toward the guide, then at the pipes above them, then back at his father.

“Fine.”

Martin nodded toward the camera. “And get the room before we start moving things.”

Eli smiled. “You’re giving me setup notes now?”

“I’m trying not to spend tomorrow arguing about where the chairs were.”

“Deal.”

The guide opened the staff door for them.

Martin went through first, then stopped on the public side and held it while Eli followed with the camera still running.

This time Eli did not have to ask whether he was coming upstairs.

DA-004 — Scene 04 — One, Then Two — Approved Draft v1.1

By the time they got back to the Rider Room, the fourth-floor corridor had thinned to the occasional closing door and the soft roll of suitcase wheels near the elevators. Martin carried the tripod while Eli unlocked the room. Neither of them mentioned the scrape in the employee passage until the door shut behind them.

Eli set the camera on the dresser and looked around as if the room had become a work site while they were gone.

“Same deal as downstairs,” Martin said. “No names.”

“I said fine.”

“You said fine while the guide was standing there.”

Eli gave him a look, then reached for the fan control beneath the window. “No cowboy. No children. No woman from the hall.”

“Good.”

The fan wound down. Its low rush faded slowly enough that Martin could hear what remained: rain against the glass, plumbing somewhere beyond the bathroom wall, the ventilation system’s thinner hiss, a television murmuring through another room and then going quiet. Eli stood in the middle of the carpet and turned once with the camera, taking in the door, bathroom, bed, two chairs by the window, and the round table between them.

“Before we touch anything,” he said toward the lens. “Entrance door latched. Bathroom door open. Fan off. Chairs are by the window with the table between them.”

Martin pointed at the recorder still in Eli’s bag.

“I know.”

Eli put it on the dresser several feet from the camera, checked the level bars, then stepped back. “Recorder is running.”

Martin sat in the chair nearest the wall. Eli took the other one, but only for a few seconds. He was up again almost immediately, checking the camera framing, moving the recorder an inch, looking at the bathroom door, then returning to the camera to make sure the red icon was still there.

“You’re supposed to sit still,” Martin said.

“I’m setting up.”

“You’ve set it up three times.”

Eli finally sat.

For the first minute neither of them spoke. A car passed below, tires pushing water along the drive. Somewhere in the hotel a door shut with enough weight to travel faintly through the wall. Martin noted it, then waited.

Eli kept his hands on his knees.

“If there’s anyone here,” he said, “make a sound we can both hear.”

Nothing followed except the rain.

Eli waited another ten seconds.

“Any sound.”

Martin watched the bathroom doorway. The light was off. The black rectangle beyond the door held the pale reflected glow from the bedside lamps and nothing else.

Eli rubbed his left forearm.

“What?” Martin asked.

“I’ve got that feeling again.”

“What feeling?”

“Cold. Not cold exactly.” Eli dragged two fingers from his wrist toward his elbow. “Like electricity under the skin.”

Martin held out his own hand between the chairs. The air felt cooler away from the vent, but not sharply so.

“I don’t have it.”

“I know.”

Eli tilted his head. “And ringing. Left ear.”

Martin listened. “I hear the vent.”

“It’s not outside.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

Eli looked toward the camera. For a moment Martin thought he was going to explain the sensation to whoever would eventually watch the footage. Instead he lowered his hand and kept quiet.

A small knock came from somewhere near the dresser.

Eli’s head snapped toward it.

Martin stayed still.

The sound had been light and hard-edged, closer to a knuckle against wood than the scrape in the passage, but the dresser, wall, bathroom door, and camera mount all occupied the same side of the room from where Martin sat.

“Did you—” Eli began.

“Wait.”

They listened.

Nothing.

Eli got up and crouched beside the recorder without touching it. The level bars were still moving. He leaned close enough to check the display, then looked toward the camera.

“Still running,” he said quietly.

Martin watched the wall behind him.

One knock sounded.

Eli froze with one hand braced on his knee.

A short pause passed.

Two knocks sounded near the bathroom side of the room.

Martin’s hand closed around the arm of the chair. His thumb slipped against the upholstery seam and caught there.

He knew the rhythm.

Not from any of the hotel stories.

From the old ghost story.

One, then two.

He had told it to Eli when Eli was a child. Years later he had admitted the story was false. Eli knew that much.

That detail had come from Martin alone.

He had invented that part too.

Eli was still looking at the recorder.

“Dad?”

Martin stood too fast. The chair legs dragged half an inch against the carpet.

Eli straightened. “What?”

Martin crossed to the bathroom and turned on the light. He checked behind the open door, then put his palm flat against the wall beside the frame.

“Did you hear that?” Eli asked.

Martin rapped the wall once with one knuckle. The sound was duller than what they had heard and stayed in the wall directly in front of him.

He tried the tile beside the bathroom entrance. Different again.

“Did you hear it?” Eli repeated.

“Yes.”

Martin stepped into the bathroom, checked the vent, then pressed one hand against the wall shared with the room. Plumbing murmured somewhere behind it. He listened until the sound faded.

Eli followed him with the camera now in his hands. “Where do you think it came from?”

“I don’t know.”

“You got up like you knew.”

Martin ignored that. He went to the room entrance, opened the door, and looked into the corridor.

A woman was walking away toward the elevators with an ice bucket tucked against her side. Farther down, a television laughed behind a closed door. No one stood immediately outside their room.

Martin crouched and looked at the gap beneath the door, then at the threshold and baseboard. He ran his fingertips along the wall beside the frame without finding anything loose.

Eli stayed inside. “You want me to ask again?”

“No.”

Martin listened into the corridor until the elevator chimed and the woman’s footsteps disappeared.

He closed the entrance door.

“What’s on the other side of that wall?” he asked.

Eli looked toward the bathroom. “Another room, I think.”

“You think?”

“I didn’t bring the floor plan.”

Martin went to the window wall and listened there. Rain struck the glass in uneven bursts. He checked the fan housing even though it was off, then the wall behind the bed. He did not know enough about the building to make any of those checks decisive. That did not matter yet. He only needed one sound he could reproduce.

Eli had stopped filming him and was watching instead.

“You’re taking this seriously.”

Martin looked back at him.

“I was taking it seriously downstairs.”

“Not like this.”

Martin turned toward the bathroom again.

A low rumble passed somewhere above or beyond the room, broad enough that Martin felt it through the wall before he placed it as sound. Eli swung the camera upward.

“There. What is that?”

“Different.”

“Different from what?”

Martin did not answer.

Eli pointed the camera at the ceiling. The image hunted in and out of focus on the small screen. He tapped the side of the camera with one finger, frowned, then lowered it.

“Hold on.”

The red recording icon was gone.

“When did that stop?” Martin asked.

“I don’t know.” Eli pressed the record button. “It was running.”

“Battery?”

“Fine.”

“Storage?”

“Fine.”

The red icon returned.

Eli looked from the camera to Martin. “That happened right after the knocks.”

“It happened sometime after you last checked it.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes.”

Eli lowered the camera. “Do you finally think something’s in here?”

Martin looked at him.

There it was—the question Eli had brought six hours and three bags of equipment to ask. Not what made the sound. Not where it came from. Something.

“Stop talking.”

Eli’s face changed. “What?”

“Just stop talking for a minute.”

Martin turned off the bathroom light again.

They stood without prompting.

The vent hissed. Rain struck the window. Somewhere below them, something heavy rolled across a hard floor and stopped.

Thirty seconds passed.

Then another thirty.

No knocks.

Martin sat again, this time on the edge of the bed. Eli remained standing beside the dresser.

After another minute Eli said, quieter, “Can you make that sound again?”

Nothing.

He waited.

“Same sound as before.”

They heard plumbing, rain, and the faint compression of an elevator door closing somewhere down the hall.

No one spoke for a while.

Eli finally set the camera back on the dresser. “So now what?”

Martin looked at the bathroom wall, then toward the entrance.

The silence left him staring at the bathroom wall and the entrance door.

“We find out what’s around this room,” he said.

Eli’s eyebrows lifted. “You want to go looking?”

“I want to know what shares these walls, where the service routes run, and what can carry a knock from one side of this place to the other.”

“That sounds like looking.”

Martin picked up the room key from the dresser.

Eli smiled once, surprised and trying not to show it.

Some of the sounds from the night resembled ordinary noises they had found elsewhere in the building.

The distant voice-like recording still did not contain words he could defend.

The final knocks were in the file.

So was their disagreement about where those knocks had come from.

The file preserved the impacts.

It did not preserve an explanation.

The transfer finished.

Eli turned the laptop toward Martin.

“Sent.”

Martin checked his phone.

A moment later he nodded.

“Got it.”

Eli closed the computer.

They left the Rider Room with their bags and the camera between them.

Neither of them named what had made the rhythm.
